### Facts:
A complaint for adultery was filed by Andres Bondoc against Guadalupe Zapata, his wife, and Dalmacio Bondoc, her paramour, for cohabiting and having repeated sexual intercourse during the period from the year 1946 to 14 March 1947, the date of the filing of the complaint, Dalmacio Bondoc knowing his codefendant to be a married woman.

The defendant-wife entered a plea of guilty and was sentenced to suffer four months of arresto mayor which penalty she served.

On 17 September 1948, the offended husband filed another complaint for adulterous acts committed by his wife and her paramour from 15 March 1947 to 17 September 1948, the date of the filing of the second complaint.

On 21 February 1949, each of the defendants filed a motion to quash the complaint on the ground that they would be twice put in jeopardy of punishment for the same offense.

The lower court sided with the defendants and quashed the second complaint.

### Issue:
Was there double jeopardy? NO

### Ruling: 
"Adultery is a crime of result and not of tendency ... it is an instantaneous crime which is consummated and exhausted or completed at the moment of the carnal union. Each sexual intercourse constitutes a crime of adultery (Cuello Calon, Derecho Penal, Vol. II, p. 569)." 

"Even if the husband should pardon his adulterous wife, such pardon would not exempt the wife and her paramour from criminal liability for adulterous acts committed after the pardon was granted because the pardon refers to previous and not to subsequent adulterous acts (Viada [5th ed.] Vol. 5, p. 208; Groizard [2nd ed.] Vol. 5, pp. 57-58)." 

There is no constitutional or legal provision which bars the filing of as many complaints for adultery as there were adulterous acts committed, each constituting one crime.

A second complaint charging the commission of adulterous acts not included in the first complaint does not constitute a violation of the double jeopardy clause of the Constitution, otherwise the adultery committed by the male defendant charged in the second complaint, should he be absolved from, or acquitted of, the first charge upon the evidence that he did not know that his codefendant was a married woman, would remain or go unpunished. The defense set up by him against the first charge upon which he was acquitted would no longer be available, because at the time of the commission of the crime charged in the second complaint, he already knew that his codefendant was a married woman and yet he continued to have carnal knowledge of her.

Even if the husband should pardon his adulterous wife, such pardon would not exempt the wife and her paramour from criminal liability for adulterous acts committed after the pardon was granted, because the pardon refers to previous, and not to subsequent, adulterous acts.

The commission of a second or subsequent act of adultery is not necessary to perfect or complete the first offense. As such, the intent to commit the crime is renewed with every sexual act.

The Court clarified that an ongoing adulterous relationship is a series of separate crimes, each subject to its own prosecution. This ruling effectively barred adulterers from using a successful defense against one charge to escape punishment for subsequent acts. 

### Ratio decidendi:
Adultery is an instantaneous crime consummated at the moment of carnal union, meaning that each distinct act of sexual intercourse constitutes an independent and complete offense. 

In the law on legal separation and marital offenses, condonation is the forgiveness of a past matrimonial wrong. Condonation or pardon operates retrospectively—it applies exclusively to past infractions known to the offended spouse and cannot be prospective. Forgiveness of prior acts of marital infidelity does not confer a continuing license or immunity to commit future acts of infidelity. Consequently, an offended spouse's condonation of past adulterous conduct does not bar an action grounded on new, distinct acts of sexual infidelity committed subsequent to the condonation.
