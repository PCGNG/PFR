### Facts:
On March 16, 1926, the accused Rodolfo A. Schneckenburger married the complainant Elena Ramirez Cartagena and after seven years of martial life, they agreed, for reason of alleged incompatibility of character, to live separately each other and on May 25, 1935 they executed a document. 

Schneckenburger, w/o leaving the Philippines, secured a decree of divorce from the civil court of Juarez, Bravos District, State of Chihuahua, Mexico. He married Julia Medel and lived together as husband and wife. 

Elena instituted two actions against the accused, one for bigamy in the Court of First Instance of Rizal and the other concubinage in the court of First Instance of Manila.

### Issue:
Does a spouse’s execution of a mutual separation agreement renouncing marital relations and claims against the other spouse constitute prior consent or condonation/pardon that bars an action founded on concubinage?

### Ruling: 
"We are of the opinion that the agreement above referred to contains a prior consent on the part of the complainant to the concubinage committed by the accused." 
"No logical difference can indeed be perceived between prior and subsequent consent, for in both instances as the offended party has chosen to compromise with his/her dishonor; he/she becomes unworthy to come to court and invoke its aid in the vindication of the wrong. For instance, a husband who delivers his wife to another man for adultery, is as unworthy, if not more, as where, upon acquiring knowledge of the adultery after its commission, he says or does nothing. Held: That prior consent is as effective as subsequent consent to bar the offended party from prosecuting the offense." 
"The foregoing conclusion should not be misconstrued as legalizing an agreement to do an illicit act, in violation of the law. An agreement between husband and wife to live separately and to marry or live in concubinage or adultery with another is contrary to law, morals and good customs and is void ab initio. But the act of the husband or wife in giving his or her consent, in advance, to the infidelity of the other spouse, deprives him or her of the right to prosecute the said spouse for concubinage or adultery."

As the term "pardon" unquestionably refers to the offense after its commission, "consent" must have been intended agreeably with its ordinary usage, to refer to the offense prior to its commission. No logical difference can indeed be perceived between prior and subsequent consent, for in both instances as the offended party has chosen to compromise with his/her dishonor, he/she becomes unworthy to come to court and invoke its aid in the vindication of the wrong. 

### Ratio decidendi:
Under Article 56(1) and (2) of the Family Code, legal separation shall be denied where the aggrieved spouse has condoned or consented to the marital offense.

Although an extrajudicial separation agreement permitting spouses to live independently and commit marital infidelity is contrary to public policy and void ab initio, it remains legally operative as factual proof of prior consent. In the contemplation of the law on marital offenses, there is no substantive difference between prior consent and subsequent condonation/pardon. In either situation, the offended spouse who voluntarily compromises their marital honor becomes unworthy to seek redress from the courts. Consequently, prior consent granted in a separation agreement completely bars the granting of legal separation or the prosecution of marital infidelity based on those consensual acts.
