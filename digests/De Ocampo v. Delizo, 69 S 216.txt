Facts
Nicolas Delizo married Rosa Villasfer in 1891 without a marriage settlement. During their marriage, they acquired several properties in Nueva Ecija and Cainta, Rizal, including 16 hectares of Cainta lands that were still being developed and cleared at the time Rosa died in 1909. Nicolas and Rosa had three children: Urbana, Severino, and Francisco. No liquidation of the conjugal properties of the first marriage was ever conducted after Rosa's death.

Two years later, in 1911, Nicolas married Dorotea De Ocampo, with whom he had nine children. During this second marriage, the remaining 51 hectares of the Cainta lands were cleared, developed, and registered in Nicolas's name, and additional properties were acquired in Nueva Ecija and Manila. Upon Nicolas's death in 1957, Dorotea and her children filed an action for partition against Nicolas’s children by his first marriage (Urbana, Severino, and the heirs of Francisco). The children of the first marriage claimed that all the Cainta properties belonged exclusively to the first marriage because their parents had initiated their acquisition, and that the fruits of those properties funded the acquisitions of the second marriage.

---

Issue

How should properties acquired and developed across two successive marriages without a prior liquidation of the first marriage be divided and liquidated between the heirs of the first marriage and the surviving spouse and heirs of the second marriage?

---

Ruling

**By determining the respective contributions and duration of each marriage in proportion to the capital and labor invested, rather than forfeiting the entire estate in favor of the first marriage.**

Quoting the Supreme Court’s legal basis verbatim:

> "Under Article 1431 of the Old Civil Code (now Article 189 of the New Civil Code), whenever the liquidation of the conjugal partnership of two or more marriages contracted by the same person should be carried out simultaneously, in order to determine the capital of each partnership every kind of proof in the absence of inventories shall be admitted; and in case of doubt, the partnership property shall be divided between or among the different partnerships in proportion to the duration of each and to the property belonging to the respective spouses."
> "The fruits of the paraphernal or exclusive property of each spouse, as well as the fruits of the conjugal property of the first marriage that remained unliquidated upon the death of the first wife, do not automatically make the properties subsequently acquired during the second marriage belong exclusively to the first marriage. What belongs to the first marriage is only the value of the property existing at the time of its dissolution, together with the fruits thereof prior to the second marriage, while the fruits produced during the second marriage and the properties acquired through the joint labor and industry of the spouses in the second marriage belong to the second conjugal partnership."
> "Where, as in this case, the homestead or real property was only partially cultivated and improved during the first marriage and was fully cleared, developed, and titled during the second marriage through the joint efforts and capital of the husband and the second wife, equity and justice require that the property be apportioned between the two marriages in proportion to the capital, labor, and duration of each."

*(Note on Governing Law: While reviewed under the topic of the Absolute Community of Property under the Family Code, the Supreme Court applied **Article 1431 of the Spanish Civil Code** and **Article 189 of the New Civil Code**. Under the **Family Code**, this exact principle is codified in **Article 104** for the **Absolute Community of Property** and **Article 131** for the **Conjugal Partnership of Gains**, which mandate that whenever the liquidation of the community/conjugal properties of two or more marriages contracted by the same person before the effectivity of the Family Code is carried out simultaneously, the respective capital, fruits, and income of each community/partnership shall be determined upon such proof as may be considered according to the rules of evidence, and in case of doubt as to which community/partnership the existing properties belong, the same shall be divided between the different communities/partnerships in proportion to the capital and duration of each. Note also that for marriages celebrated under the Family Code, **Article 103** strictly requires the surviving spouse to liquidate the community property within one year from the death of the deceased spouse; otherwise, a mandatory regime of **complete separation of property** governs any subsequent marriage.)*

---

### **Provisions and Doctrines Relied Upon**

* **Article 1431 of the Spanish Civil Code / Article 189 of the New Civil Code** (now **Article 104** for Absolute Community of Property and **Article 131** for Conjugal Partnership of Gains under the **Family Code**): Governs the simultaneous liquidation of the marital properties of two or more successive marriages contracted by the same person, requiring proof of respective capital, fruits, and income, and dividing doubtful properties in proportion to the capital and duration of each marriage.
* **Article 1401 of the Spanish Civil Code / Article 153 of the New Civil Code** (parallel to **Article 91** and **Article 117 of the Family Code**): Classifies property acquired during the marriage through the industry, labor, or onerous title at the expense of the common fund as marital property.
* **Doctrine of Proportional Apportionment in Successive Marriages:** When real property (such as a homestead or raw land) is initiated during a first marriage but cleared, cultivated, and perfected into titled ownership during a second marriage without prior liquidation, the property is apportioned equitably between the two marriages based on the extent of improvement, capital, and duration of each marriage rather than awarded entirely to the first marriage.

---

Ratio

When a surviving spouse remarries without liquidating the marital property of the first marriage, and both estates must later be liquidated simultaneously, the law rejects an all-or-nothing award to the first marriage. Under Article 1431 of the Old Civil Code and Article 189 of the New Civil Code (now mirrored in Article 104 of the Family Code for the Absolute Community of Property), the respective capital, fruits, and contributions of each marriage must be established by competent evidence, and in case of doubt, the properties are divided between the two marriages in proportion to the capital and duration of each. Because only 16 hectares of the Cainta lands were cultivated during Nicolas's 18-year first marriage with Rosa, whereas the remaining 51 hectares were cleared, developed, and titled during his 46-year second marriage with Dorotea through their joint efforts, the Cainta lands and subsequent acquisitions must be apportioned proportionally between the first and second marriages rather than adjudicated solely to the heirs of the first wife.

---

Fallo

> "WHEREFORE, the decision of the Court of Appeals is hereby modified in the sense that the properties of the two marriages of the late Nicolas Delizo shall be partitioned in accordance with the rules on simultaneous liquidation under Article 189 of the New Civil Code, as set forth in the body of this decision, and the records of these cases are remanded to the trial court for further proceedings to determine the exact partition and accounting in accordance with this decision. Without pronouncement as to costs.
> SO ORDERED."
