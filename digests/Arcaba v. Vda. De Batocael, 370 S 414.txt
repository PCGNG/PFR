Araneta v. Concepcion
G.R. No. L-9667 | 99 Phil. 709 | July 31, 1956 | Labrador, J.

I. Facts
Luis Ma. Araneta filed an action for legal separation against his wife, Emma Benitez Araneta, before the Court of First Instance (CFI) of Manila, Branch VI, presided over by Judge Hermogenes Concepcion, on the ground of adultery.   


After issues were joined, the wife filed an omnibus petition supported by affidavits seeking:

Custody of their three minor children;

Monthly alimony pendente lite of ₱5,000 for herself and the children;

Return of her passport;

Injunctive relief against the petitioner and his agents; and

Attorney’s fees.   


The husband opposed the omnibus petition, denying the accusations against him, disputing the valuation of the conjugal assets, alleging that the wife had abandoned the family home and children, and praying that the court receive oral evidence before ruling on the incidents.   


The trial judge refused to admit evidence on the omnibus petition, citing the mandatory six-month cooling-off period under Article 103 of the Civil Code (which prohibited trying an action for legal separation within six months from the filing of the petition). The judge reasoned that introducing evidence during this period would inflame passions, defeat the legislative policy favoring marital reconciliation, and run counter to traditional Filipino family values. Proceeding solely on the basis of the wife’s affidavits and documents, the trial court issued an order granting her custody of the children, awarding ₱2,300 per month as support, ₱300 monthly for house rent, and ₱2,000 for attorney’s fees.   


The husband filed a petition for certiorari and mandamus before the Supreme Court to annul the order and compel the trial court to receive evidence.   


II. Issue
Whether Article 103 of the Civil Code (the six-month cooling-off period) bars the trial court from hearing and admitting evidence on provisional matters, such as custody of minor children and support pendente lite, in an action for legal separation.   


III. Held
No. The prohibition in Article 103 against trying an action for legal separation within six months from filing refers exclusively to the trial on the merits of the main case (i.e., whether the decree of legal separation should be granted). It does not bar the court from hearing, receiving evidence on, and determining preliminary and ancillary matters, such as the custody of children and alimony pendente lite.   


IV. Ratio Decidendi
The Supreme Court held that statutory provisions must be harmonized rather than construed in isolation:   
Jur

Scope of the Cooling-Off Period: While Article 103 establishes a six-month cooling-off period as a practical expedient to facilitate reconciliation, this legislative policy does not override other explicit statutory mandates, specifically Article 105 of the Civil Code, which directs that during the pendency of the legal separation proceedings, the custody of minor children and support must be determined by the court according to the circumstances.

Necessity of Evidence: Determining the welfare of minor children and the reasonable amount of support cannot be decided arbitrarily or based on unexamined affidavits when factual assertions are vigorously contested. Closing judicial eyes to actual facts and refusing to receive evidence can cause manifest injustice.   
Jur

Distinction Between Ancillary Relief and Main Merits: Receiving evidence to determine provisional custody and support does not amount to a trial of the legal separation action itself. The court must ascertain facts sufficient to protect the best interests of the children and calibrate support to the financial capacity of the parties without adjudicating the principal cause of action on the merits.   


V. Fallo
"The writ prayed for is hereby issued and the respondent judge or whosoever takes his place is ordered to proceed on the question of custody and support pendente lite in accordance with this opinion. The court's order fixing the alimony and requiring payment is reversed. Without costs."
